🧭Security Principles24%Risk assessment

🧭Security Principles24%Risk concepts

🧭Security Principles24%Risk treatment
Accept, avoid, mitigate, transfer. Expect several questions that describe a decision and ask which one it was.
🧭Security Principles24%Security controls
Two independent axes — type and function. Almost every control question is asking about one of them, and the exam rarely tells you which.

🚨Security Governance17.3%Disaster recovery

🚨Security Governance17.3%Measuring cybersecurity effectiveness
Key metrics, key risk indicators, dashboards, scorecards and reports — the vocabulary of proving a security programme is doing its job.
🚨Security Governance17.3%RTO, RPO and MTD
The highest-value page in this domain. RTO looks forward to recovery; RPO looks backward to your last good data.
🚨Security Governance17.3%Security awareness training
Awareness, training and education are three different things with three different purposes. And this is the standing answer to social engineering.
🚪IAM Concepts20%Access control fundamentals
Short, and everything else in this domain is written in its vocabulary. Get subject and object the right way round and the rest follows.
🚪IAM Concepts20%DAC, MAC, RBAC and ABAC
The single highest-value page in Domain 3. Expect several questions that describe a scenario and ask which model it is.
🚪IAM Concepts20%Identity lifecycle
Access has a beginning, a middle and an end. The middle is where privilege creep happens and the end is where orphaned accounts come from.
🚪IAM Concepts20%Least privilege and segregation of duties
Least privilege is the most frequently correct principle in this domain. Segregation of duties is the specific answer to fraud.
🚪IAM Concepts20%Logical access controls
Logical controls restrict access to systems and data. The examined skill is placing a control on the right side of the physical/logical line.
🚪IAM Concepts20%Privileged access
What makes an account privileged, why attackers want one above all else, and the control set the exam expects around them.
🌐Networking and Cloud Security Concepts21.3%Cloud and virtualisation
The shared responsibility model is the most examined idea here. The rule that resolves it: the more the provider manages, the less you do — but the data is always yours.
🌐Networking and Cloud Security Concepts21.3%Common attacks
The attack catalogue. Most questions describe a scenario and ask you to name it, so the distinguishing detail of each one is what matters.
🌐Networking and Cloud Security Concepts21.3%Defence in depth
The principle that ties the whole domain together. The examined subtlety is that layers must be independent — and that more controls is not the same as more depth.
🌐Networking and Cloud Security Concepts21.3%Embedded systems, ICS and IoT
Why embedded devices, industrial control systems, and consumer IoT get their own line on the live outline — they break the usual security assumptions.
🌐Networking and Cloud Security Concepts21.3%IP addressing
Recognise a private address range on sight, and say what NAT, DHCP and DNS each do. That is most of the marks here.
🌐Networking and Cloud Security Concepts21.3%Network defence devices
The highest-traffic distinction in Domain 4 is IDS versus IPS. Answer the textbook, not your deployment experience.
🌐Networking and Cloud Security Concepts21.3%Network fundamentals
Network types, topologies, and the device list. Mostly recall — and the hub-versus-switch distinction is a security question, not a performance one.
🌐Networking and Cloud Security Concepts21.3%Network threats
Threat actor types, the insider problem, and the malware taxonomy — where virus, worm and Trojan are separated by how they spread, not by what they do.
🌐Networking and Cloud Security Concepts21.3%The OSI and TCP/IP models
The highest-leverage page in this domain. A large share of Domain 4 questions reduce to "which layer is this?" — for a protocol, a device, or an attack.
🌐Networking and Cloud Security Concepts21.3%Ports and protocols
Pure memorisation, and therefore free marks. Learn the table until recall is instant, then learn which protocols have a secure replacement.
🌐Networking and Cloud Security Concepts21.3%Segmentation and the DMZ
Why a flat network is dangerous, what a DMZ is for, and the three-word answer to most segmentation questions: limits lateral movement.
🌐Networking and Cloud Security Concepts21.3%VPNs and remote access
Site-to-site versus remote access, what a tunnel actually covers, and the wireless security ladder that sits alongside it.
🌐Networking and Cloud Security Concepts21.3%Wireless and Bluetooth
Wi-Fi security basics, and Bluetooth's own short list of named attacks — new, explicit content on the live outline.
🌐Networking and Cloud Security Concepts21.3%Zero trust
One core assumption to learn, plus the third-party agreement vocabulary — SLA, MOU, MSA — that ISC2 groups into this part of the domain.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%Asset lifecycle and end-of-life
Asset protection means knowing what you have, tracking it through its life, and retiring it before it becomes unsupported and unpatchable.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%Configuration management
Inventory comes first — you cannot protect what you do not know you have. Then change control, with a backout plan every time.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%Data classification
The roles question — owner, custodian, user — is asked more than anything else here. Owner decides; custodian implements.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%Data handling
Three states, and disposal methods with precise definitions. The state you forget is data in use — and it is the one encryption struggles with.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%Encryption concepts
One of the heaviest topics in this domain. The key-direction rule — public encrypts, private decrypts — answers a surprising number of questions.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%Event triage and threat intelligence
Security event triage — prioritisation and correlation — plus the vocabulary of threat actors, cyber threat intelligence, and threat frameworks.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%Hashing and integrity
"Hashing is not encryption" is the sentence this topic exists to teach. Passwords are hashed, never encrypted.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%The incident response plan
Learn the phase order cold. Then remember that the correct FIRST action in any incident scenario is to follow the plan — not to fix anything.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%Incident terminology
Short and entirely definitional. Every event is not an incident, and every incident is not a breach.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%Logging and monitoring
Detective controls. Logging without review detects nothing — which is the distinction the exam keeps returning to.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%Physical penetration testing
One of security testing's three families under the live outline — authorised attempts to walk, talk or trick your way past physical controls.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%Quantum-resistant cryptography
One idea, at CC depth: today's public-key encryption has a known future weakness, and the industry is already replacing it. No maths required.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%Security policies
Mostly recognition: match a described situation to the policy that governs it. AUP and BYOD carry the most questions.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%Security testing methods
Two of the outline's three security testing clusters: readiness testing (team colours) and application testing (scanning and analysis techniques). The third, physical testing, has its own page.
⚙️Security Operations and Incident Response17.3%System hardening
Baselines, patching and least functionality. The sentence to hold: a service that is not running cannot be exploited.
